Don’t retrain — give it the documentsScript · 03 / 22

So the industry needed a way to give a frozen model knowledge it was never trained on. The answer it converged on is called Retrieval-Augmented Generation — RAG.

One sentence: do not retrain the model — give it the documents.

Four steps. One, the user asks a question. Two, we take that question and search a library of documents for the passages related to it. Three, we paste those passages next to the question. Four, the model answers from the text in front of it, instead of from memory.

You can see why industry adopted it: no retraining cost, private data stays private, every answer traces back to a source, and when the documents change the system's knowledge changes with them.

[Point at box 2. You turn the spotlight on it next slide.]

One detail about step two, and it will matter enormously later. There are two ways to search a library. You can match the words — find documents containing the same terms. Or match the meaning — represent question and documents as mathematical objects and find the ones that sit close together, even with no shared words. Both are used. Please remember there are two.

What we built, and how we score itScript · 06 / 22

So the first thing we did was build baselines — one of each of the two kinds of search I just mentioned.

The word-matcher is BM25. It counts how many of the query's terms appear in each passage, weighting rare terms more heavily than common ones. It is almost thirty years old and it is still the standard everything gets measured against.

The meaning-matcher is mDPR — a neural network that maps text into a vector, so passages whose meaning is close to the question land close to it in that space, even when they share no words. It is multilingual, and Arabic is one of its languages.

We evaluated on MIRACL Arabic: 2,896 questions over 2.06 million Wikipedia passages, with human judgements of which passages are relevant. That last part matters — the ground truth is human, not automatic.

And the score is NDCG at 10. It asks not just whether the right passage came back, but how high up. Rank one is worth more than rank ten. Zero to one, higher is better.

That is the right measure here, because it is not enough for the passage to be somewhere in the list. It has to be near the top, where the language model will actually read it.

Where is the fault? Three suspectsScript · 08 / 22

A third of our questions fail. The obvious next question — and it is the question that organised the entire project — is: where is the fault? There are only three places it can be. We tested all three.

Suspect one, the search engine. We tested both kinds. Word matching scored 0.4621, meaning matching 0.4993. Both stuck around one half. But here is the informative part — they do not fail on the same questions. Merge their two result lists and the score jumps to 0.6267, far above either alone. Each finds what the other misses.

So: two independent algorithms, one statistical and thirty years old, one neural — both stuck at one half. That is not a bug in one method. Something limits both from outside. Acquitted.

Suspect two, the library. Maybe the answers simply are not there. We audited the corpus against the benchmark's own answer key. Every required passage is present. And for ninety percent of questions the right passage is reachable if we look a hundred deep. The knowledge is in the library — the system just cannot reach it. Acquitted.

Suspect three, the question. We sorted every question by length. The shortest — one to three words — scored 0.345, far below every other group. Guilty.

The fault is in the questionScript · 09 / 22

And once you look at what we are actually asking the system to do, it stops being surprising.

A typical question is four words. Four words, to distinguish one passage from two million. And the passage that answers it is a hundred words, written by somebody who never saw the question, using their own vocabulary, for their own purposes, possibly decades ago.

[Beat.]

The fault is not in the search engine. It is in the question. It is too poor to search with.

This has a name — vocabulary mismatch. The question and the document describe the same thing in different words. And Arabic makes it worse in three specific ways.

Morphology. Arabic is built on roots and patterns — one root generates dozens of surface forms. The user writes one; the document uses another form of the same root. To a system matching words, those are unrelated strings.

Orthography. Alif with hamza, alif without, alif with hamza below. Ta marbuta and ha. Alif maqsura and ya. The same word, both spellings acceptable, two different byte sequences. No match.

Diglossia. People ask questions the way they speak — in dialect. Encyclopedias are written in Modern Standard Arabic. A systematic gap between how questions are asked and how knowledge is written down.

The circleScript · 10 / 22

That diagnosis determines the fix. It is not a choice we made — it is forced by where the fault is. If the defect is in the question, repair the question, before it reaches the search engine. Not the retriever. Not the index.

And that has a practical consequence worth stating: the fix becomes a module you place in front of an existing system. Nothing downstream is touched, nothing retrained, two million passages not re-indexed. You can add it to a running system and remove it again.

So — what does repairing the question mean, concretely? If the problem is that question and document use different words, then it means one thing precisely: the question needs to contain the document's words. Not more words in general. Not better-phrased words. The words the answering document actually uses.

A plausible document — Query2DocScript · 11 / 22

Here is how the literature breaks that circle. It is a genuinely clever move, and it is not ours.

We do not need the real document. We need a plausible one.

Think about what we actually need. We need the vocabulary the answering document uses — not the document itself. And a language model that has read enormous quantities of Arabic can write what such a document would probably say. Its guess will share vocabulary with the real one, even when it gets details wrong.

So the model does not have to be right. It only has to produce the right kind of words.

The technique is called Query2Doc. Before searching, ask the model to write the answer it thinks is correct, glue that text onto the original question, and search with both. Four words becomes eighty, and many of the extra words are the ones the real document uses.

And be clear about one thing, because it surprises people: nobody ever reads that generated text. It is not shown to a user. It is not an answer. It is raw vocabulary, and it goes straight into the search box.

Structural note

The circle is broken here by guessing a document. On slide 16, CSQE breaks the same circle by fetching real ones. Same circle, two breaks, the second better — that symmetry is the backbone of the talk.

Adaptation 1 · query repetition
It helped one retriever and harmed the otherScript · 13 / 22

The first answer was encouraging. On the meaning-matching retriever, query expansion took us from 0.4993 to 0.6164. A large improvement, from an eight-billion-parameter open model on a free GPU. It held for every viable model we tested.

But on the word-matching retriever we got the opposite result. Expansion made it worse — for six of the nine models.

The reason is dilution. BM25 scores by term overlap. Add eighty generated words to a four-word question, and the original four — the ones that carried the meaning — are now a small fraction of the query. Their weight in the scoring collapses. The expansion drowns the question.

Our fix was simple: repeat the original question several times inside the expanded query, to restore its weight. That worked, and it recovered all nine models.

[Beat — then flag the deeper finding.]

But hold onto the finding itself, because it is the first sign of something bigger: the two ways of searching did not respond to expansion the same way. One was helped. One was harmed. I will come back to that.

And note what has already happened to our research question. Taking the English technique and applying it directly did not work. It needed an adaptation. That is the first of three.

The muftiScript · 16 / 22

So what should it have done instead? It answered from memory. It should have looked first.

Our supervisor's consultant gave us an analogy for exactly this, and it fits so well I want to use his own words: a standard language model is like a layperson — it tries to answer generatively.

Ask a layperson a question of fiqh and he answers from what he remembers. He sounds certain. He may be right, he may be badly wrong, and he sounds the same either way.

Ask a mufti and he does something different. His general knowledge tells him roughly where the answer lives — but he does not rule from memory. He takes the book off the shelf, finds the passage, and quotes its actual wording before he gives the ruling. He is not more intelligent. He has one extra step: he looks before he speaks.

And we did not simply accept that analogy because it came from a consultant. We turned it into a research question and tested it — and the analogy has two halves.

The first half is "know which chapter to open" — teach the system the structure of the library. We surveyed eight research directions, about twenty-five papers, three families of method in depth. Every method that does this is an index-time method: it requires rebuilding the index over all two million passages, with no incremental path. Contextual Retrieval, three to twenty thousand dollars. RAPTOR, two hundred and sixteen — affordable, but its gains are on multi-hop questions and ours are factoid. Title prepending, cheap, but it lost 11.6% on one dataset. We ruled that half out on cost and risk, and we documented the cost.

The second half — "open the book instead of guessing" — costs one extra retrieval pass and nothing else. That is the half we built.

CSQE — three stepsScript · 17 / 22

The method is called Corpus-Steered Query Expansion — CSQE — and it is three steps.

One: look. Search once with the bare question and take back the top five documents. These are not the answer. This is a cheap first glance, and some of them will be wrong.

Two: read. Extract from those five the sentences that genuinely match the question.

Three: expand. Use those sentences as the expansion — words copied from documents actually in our library, not words invented by a model.

Back to our question. Here is what the first glance returned: the real grammar article, from our corpus, containing أب، أخ، حم، فو، ذو. The expansion now carries the correct vocabulary — not because the model knew it, but because it was copied from a document that had it. Score: one point zero. Rank one.

Which gives the difference in two lines. Blind expansion asks the model: what do you think this is? Corpus-steered expansion asks the library: what do you actually have?

[Beat. This is the most quotable moment in the talk. Say it slowly and add nothing after it.]

And the library cannot hallucinate. It only contains what it contains.

Placement — the contributionScript · 18 / 22

Now I come back to the detail I planted at the beginning: there are two ways to search a library, and they fail on different questions. So the natural move is to run both and merge. That scored 0.6267 — with no expansion of any kind. Far above either alone. We set that as the number we had to beat: not the weak baseline, the strong one.

Then we did the obvious thing. We had a technique that improves retrieval and two retrievers, so we gave the expansion to both.

[Beat. Deadpan.]

It made things worse. Same technique, same fusion, same models. The only variable is which retriever receives the expansion. Meaning-matcher only: 0.6474. Both: 0.6936. Word-matcher only, withholding it from the meaning-matcher entirely: 0.7137.

Why — and this room will recognise the mechanism faster than most. The word-matcher scores by counting term hits: more terms, more chances to hit. The meaning-matcher compresses the whole input into a single representation — one average meaning. Bolt eighty generated words onto a four-word question and the question no longer dominates that average.

It behaves like a matched filter: best when the reference is a clean template of the signal you want. Padding the reference with generated text is adding noise to the template, and the match degrades. And notice — this is the same asymmetry from slide 13. It was there from the start; we just did not understand it yet.

But there is a deeper reason, and it is the one to remember. Fusion only pays off while the two retrievers remain different. Their entire value is that they fail on different questions. Feed the same eighty words into both and they start to look alike — same documents, same mistakes. The complementarity we were merging for disappears.

So the finding is not "expansion is good" or "expansion is bad." It is that expansion has a place, and the place matters — apply it to the sparse retriever and withhold it from the dense one, precisely to protect the difference between them.

So let me answer the question I set at the beginning. Can we take these English techniques off the shelf, apply them to Arabic, and will they work?

Yes — but not unmodified. Applied directly, off the shelf, they failed. Three adaptations were required, and each came out of a failure we measured.

One: query repetition. Expansion diluted the sparse retriever and made it worse for six of nine models. Repeating the original question inside the expanded query restored its weight.

Two: corpus grounding. Blind generation hallucinated on Arabic-specific terms — you saw it do exactly that. Grounding the expansion in retrieved documents made it reliable.

Three: asymmetric placement. Expanding both retrievers destroyed the complementarity fusion depends on. The expansion belongs on the sparse retriever only.

[Beat, then land the meta-point.]

And I would put it this way: if these techniques had transferred unchanged, there would have been no thesis here. The fact that they did not is the finding.

LimitsScript · 21 / 22

Finally, I want to state the limits of this work myself, rather than leave them to be found.

We evaluated on one benchmark — Arabic Wikipedia — in Modern Standard Arabic only. We did not test dialect, and given that diglossia was one of the three problems I named at the start, that is a real gap.

We used one pair of retrievers and one language. We have not shown these findings transfer further.

We measured retrieval quality, not final answer quality. That was deliberate — retrieval is the ceiling — but it does mean we have not demonstrated the downstream improvement directly.

Our best generator, Aya Expanse, is licensed CC-BY-NC. Fine for research; it rules out commercial deployment without a different model.

CSQE costs an extra retrieval pass for every query. It is not free.

And it has a failure mode we can name precisely: when the first glance returns poisoned documents, the expansion is grounded in the wrong thing and the query gets worse. That happened to 131 questions — about 4.5%. We measured it, and it is the clearest direction for future work.

Prepared answers — rehearse, do not improviseScript · 22 / 22

“Where is the generation? This is only half a RAG system.”
A RAG system's answer is bounded by what it retrieves. If the right passage never comes back, no generator can recover it. We worked on the ceiling. (Already delivered on slide 5 — this is the fallback.)

“Why didn't you evaluate final answer quality?”
It would measure the generator, not our contribution. Answer quality depends on the model, the prompt and the judge. Retrieval metrics isolate what we changed. Our supervisor's guidance was explicit: get retrieval right and generation follows.

“Why only Modern Standard Arabic?”
The benchmark we needed — human relevance judgements at this scale — exists only in MSA. MIRACL gave us 2,896 judged questions over 2.06M passages. No dialect equivalent exists. Building one is future work.

“Isn't this just prompt engineering?”
The prompt is one component. The findings are not about prompts: that expansion dilutes sparse retrieval and repetition repairs it; that grounding beats generation; that placement changes the result by 0.066. None is a prompt choice, and they hold across ten models.

“Why not RAPTOR / the structure-aware approach?”
Scope, not complexity. RAPTOR costs about $216 — affordable. But its gains are on multi-hop questions and MIRACL is mostly factoid. Every structure-aware method also requires rebuilding the index over 2M passages with no incremental path.

“Is this actually new?”
Closest prior art is Exp4Fuse, ACL 2025 Findings — QE on a single sparse retriever, then fusion. Our claim is the narrow one: monolingual MSA Arabic, dense–sparse hybrid.

“How do you know the example wasn't cherry-picked?”
It was chosen to be clear, and I said so when I showed it. The claim rests on the aggregate: 56.8% of 2,896 questions improved, mean +0.189.

“Does a bigger model always do better?”
No — and be careful. Chapter 5 qualifies this heavily. Do not claim a clean relationship in either direction.
